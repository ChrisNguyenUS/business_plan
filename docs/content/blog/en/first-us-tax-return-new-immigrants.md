When you first arrive in the U.S., the first tax question isn't "how much do I file?" but **"how does the IRS classify me?"** A **resident** is taxed on worldwide income and files Form 1040. A **nonresident** is taxed only on U.S. income and files Form 1040-NR. Which one you are decides almost everything else.

> **Key takeaways**
> - You are a **resident** for tax purposes if you pass **either** the *green card test* or the *substantial presence test*. If you pass neither, you are a nonresident.
> - **Residents** are taxed on **worldwide income**, including wages, bank interest and rent from Vietnam.
> - **The year you arrive is often "dual-status"**: part of the year as a nonresident and the rest as a resident.
> - An **SSN** is the number you use if you are eligible to work in the U.S. An **ITIN** is only for people who must file taxes but are not eligible for an SSN.
> - Your spouse and children **need an SSN or ITIN** for you to claim them as dependents. They must also fall into an eligible group (see [Filing Taxes for Tax Year 2026](/en/blog/file-taxes-tax-year-2026)).

## Resident or nonresident for tax?

According to the IRS, someone who is not a U.S. citizen is a nonresident **unless** they pass one of the two tests below.

### The green card test

You are a resident if you were **a lawful permanent resident of the United States at any time during the calendar year** (Publication 519). Having the green card for part of the year counts.

### The substantial presence test

You are a resident if you were physically present in the U.S. on **at least 31 days this year** and **at least 183 days** under a three-year formula:

- all of your days this year, plus
- 1/3 of your days last year, plus
- 1/6 of your days the year before that.

Some days **don't count**: for example days you were in the U.S. as a **student on an F, J, M or Q visa** who is following the visa's requirements. If you exclude days for this reason, you must file **Form 8843**. Some people can also be treated as nonresidents under the "closer connection" exception. See the IRS [Substantial Presence Test](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/substantial-presence-test) page and Publication 519.

### The first year is often "dual-status"

If you can be both a nonresident and a resident in the same year, usually **the year you arrive in or leave the U.S.**, you must file a **dual-status return**. For green card holders, the date you become a resident usually counts from the first day you are present in the U.S. as a permanent resident. The IRS also has a **"first-year choice"** that lets some people be treated as residents earlier. First-year returns are easy to get wrong, so ask a preparer who knows international cases.

## How to file, by group

| You are | You usually file | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Green card holder, in the U.S. all year** | Form 1040 | Resident: report worldwide income |
| **Green card holder, arrived mid-year** | Dual-status | Income before you became a resident is handled differently |
| **F-1 or J-1 student** | Form 1040-NR and **Form 8843** | Days as a student usually don't count toward the test |
| **Working on a visa (H-1B, L-1…)** | Form 1040 or 1040-NR | Depends on the substantial presence test and your days in the U.S. |
| **U.S. citizen (including newly naturalized)** | Form 1040 | Taxed on worldwide income |

This is general information and doesn't replace a review of your own situation.

## SSN or ITIN?

- An **SSN (Social Security number)** is for people who are **allowed to work** in the U.S. It is issued by the Social Security Administration.
- An **ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number)** is an IRS number for people who **must file federal taxes but are not eligible for an SSN**. According to the IRS, green card holders and anyone eligible for an SSN do **not** use an ITIN.
- An ITIN is for **federal tax purposes only**. It doesn't give you the right to work, doesn't count for Social Security benefits, and **doesn't change your immigration status**.
- You submit **Form W-7 with your tax return**, not separately. The IRS says to allow **about 7 weeks**; it can take **9 to 11 weeks** during tax season (January 15 to April 30) or when you apply from overseas.
- If your ITIN has expired and you need to file, you must renew it before you file.

## What about a spouse or children in Vietnam?

**Children in Vietnam.** A dependent must be a **U.S. citizen, resident alien, U.S. national, or a resident of Canada or Mexico** (IRS). A child in Vietnam who doesn't fall into these groups **cannot** be claimed as your dependent. The money you send to support them doesn't change that.

**A spouse in Vietnam who is a nonresident alien.** You usually have two paths, and each affects your tax:

1. **File separately:** you file as "married filing separately" or, if you qualify, another status. Your spouse files only if they have U.S. income.
2. **Choose to treat your spouse as a resident** so you can file jointly: then **both of you are taxed on worldwide income**, and your spouse needs an SSN or ITIN.

Don't choose by gut feeling. The tax can differ a lot between the two, so run both before you file. The details are in IRS Publication 519.

## Income in Vietnam and tax already paid in Vietnam

If you are a resident, income from Vietnam goes on Form 1040. If you **already paid income tax in Vietnam** on that income, the IRS lets you choose a **foreign tax credit (Form 1116)** or a deduction. The IRS notes the credit is usually the better choice because it reduces your U.S. tax directly.

If you have **bank accounts in Vietnam**, you may have to report them too. See [FBAR and Form 8938 for Vietnam Bank Accounts](/en/blog/fbar-form-8938-vietnam-bank-accounts).

## State taxes

Texas has **no** state personal income tax, according to the Texas Comptroller. Many other states do. If you live in another state, check that state's income tax and its separate deadline.

## Frequently asked questions

### I just got my green card. How do I file this year?

If you held a green card at any time during the year, you pass the green card test and are a resident for tax purposes. If you arrived in the U.S. mid-year, that year is usually dual-status. File a return and report the correct date your residency began.

### I already have an SSN. Do I also need an ITIN?

No. People eligible for an SSN don't use an ITIN. If your spouse or child isn't eligible for an SSN but you need to claim them, they may need an ITIN.

### How long does an ITIN application take?

The IRS says about 7 weeks, and possibly 9 to 11 weeks during tax season (January 15 to April 30) or when applying from overseas. You submit Form W-7 with your tax return.

### Do I report wages I earned in Vietnam before moving to the U.S.?

It depends on the date your residency started and the type of income. Residents report worldwide income. The first year is usually dual-status, so ask a preparer before you file.

### Can I claim my child in Vietnam as a dependent?

No, unless the child is a U.S. citizen, resident alien, U.S. national, or a resident of Canada or Mexico. Sending money to support the child doesn't change that.

### I don't have an SSN yet. Can I still file?

If you're eligible for an SSN, get it first. If you aren't eligible but have income to report, you can apply for an ITIN with your tax return.
