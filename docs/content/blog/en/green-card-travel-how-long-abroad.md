A green card holder can visit family abroad (in Vietnam, for example) and return to the U.S. with a valid green card if the trip is **shorter than 1 year**. Still, USCIS describes two important thresholds:

- Being outside the U.S. for **6 months or more** may break the **continuous residence** you need to apply for citizenship.
- Being outside the U.S. for **more than 1 year** may be treated as **abandoning** permanent residence, unless you obtained a **reentry permit** before leaving.

These rules apply to both 2-year and 10-year cards.

## Trips shorter than 6 months

According to the USCIS [International Travel as a Permanent Resident](https://www.uscis.gov/green-card/after-we-grant-your-green-card/international-travel-as-a-permanent-resident) page (updated September 12, 2025), temporary or brief travel **usually does not affect** permanent resident status. You return with a **valid, unexpired green card** (Form I-551) and your passport.

A U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) officer may still ask about your trip, your job and where you live. Keep documents that show you live in the U.S.

## Trips of 6 months to under 1 year

According to the same USCIS page:

- You **can still use your green card to return**.
- But **absences of 6 months or more may disrupt the continuous residence** required for naturalization. If you plan to file Form N-400 soon, long trips can push back the date you become eligible. See [What Is Form N-400?](/en/blog/what-is-form-n-400) for the continuous residence requirement.
- USCIS also notes that a trip **shorter than 1 year can still be found to be abandonment** if the officer concludes you did not intend to make the U.S. your permanent home (for example, if you moved your home and job abroad).

## Trips longer than 1 year

- USCIS uses **more than 1 year** absent as the general guide for abandonment.
- If you know you will be outside the U.S. **for more than 1 year**, apply for a **reentry permit** (Form I-131) **before you leave**.
- If you are outside the U.S. **for more than 2 years**, the reentry permit expires. USCIS suggests applying for a **returning resident (SB-1) visa** at a U.S. Embassy or Consulate. SB-1 applicants must show they are eligible for an immigrant visa and need a medical exam.

For absences of **1 year or more** for certain kinds of work (for example, for the U.S. government or certain U.S. companies abroad), there is also **Form N-470** to preserve residence for naturalization. Ask an attorney about this case.

## What is a reentry permit?

A reentry permit is a document USCIS issues through **Form I-131**. It lets a permanent resident return to the U.S. after a long trip without being considered to have abandoned residence just because of the time abroad. According to USCIS:

- Apply **before you leave the U.S.**; the permit expires if you stay abroad **more than 2 years**.
- A permit **does not guarantee** admission; you still have to be admissible.
- Under the [G-1055 Fee Schedule](https://www.uscis.gov/g-1055) (edition October 16, 2026), the fee is **$630 (paper filing)**. The [USCIS I-131 page](https://www.uscis.gov/i-131) says reentry permits **cannot be filed online**.
- Under the Form I-131 instructions, you must be in the U.S. when you file and when you go to biometrics. Allow time for the biometrics appointment before you book your trip.

## What if I lose my green card while abroad?

If your trip is under 1 year and your card is lost, stolen or destroyed, you can file **Form I-131A** at a U.S. consulate for a document to board your flight home. Details are in [Lost Your Green Card in the U.S. or Abroad?](/en/blog/lost-green-card).

## Good habits for long trips abroad

- **Keep your green card valid.** If it will expire while you are away, renew it before you leave. See [Renewing a 10-Year Green Card](/en/blog/renew-green-card-i-90).
- **Keep your ties to the U.S.:** a home address, bank accounts, a job, and tax returns filed as a U.S. resident.
- **Write down the departure and return dates** of every trip. Form N-400 asks about every trip in the last 5 years.
- **2-year card holders:** don't miss the 90-day Form I-751 window. See [2-Year to 10-Year Green Card](/en/blog/remove-conditions-green-card-i-751).

Every situation is different. If you have been, or will be, outside the U.S. for a long time, talk to a licensed immigration attorney before you go.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long can a green card holder stay outside the U.S.?

Trips under 1 year usually return with a valid green card. Six months or more may break continuous residence for citizenship. More than 1 year may be treated as abandoning residence, so you need a reentry permit obtained before leaving.

### Can a 2-year green card holder travel abroad?

Yes. A 2-year card follows the same travel rules as a 10-year card. Just make sure you file Form I-751 in the 90 days before the card expires.

### Will I lose my green card if I stay abroad for 7 months?

A trip under 1 year usually doesn't cost you your card, but 6 months or more may affect continuous residence for citizenship, and an officer may ask about your intent to live permanently in the U.S.

### How long is a reentry permit valid?

According to USCIS, the permit expires if you stay outside the U.S. for more than 2 years. You must apply before you leave.

### How much does a reentry permit cost?

Under the G-1055 Fee Schedule (edition October 16, 2026): $630, filed on paper. Fees can change, so check again before you file.

### I've been abroad more than 2 years. How do I return?

USCIS suggests applying for a returning resident (SB-1) visa at a U.S. Embassy or Consulate. You must show you are eligible and have a medical exam.

### Do long trips affect my citizenship application?

They can. Absences of 6 months or more may break continuous residence, so you may have to wait longer before you can file Form N-400.
